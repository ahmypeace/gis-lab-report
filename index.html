GIS Lab Portfolio

Fifteen weeks of GIS problem solving at Temple University, from finding a site for a healthy food store in Philadelphia to comparing crime and Airbnb activity across Chicago.

About This Portfolio

Peace Amaka Onebunne is a PhD student in communication at Temple University. This course treated GIS as a way to solve problems, and the three maps featured here each answer one: where a new store should go, how to compare districts of different sizes, and how the choice of map units changes what a map shows.

Map 1 · Site selection

Where Should a Healthy Food Store Go?

The task was to find land in Philadelphia for a new healthy food store that people could reach and that would not compete with existing healthy food outlets. Qualifying land had to sit inside an Empowerment Zone, within 2,000 feet of a SEPTA subway or Regional Rail station, and outside 1,200 feet of any Farmers Market or Healthy Corner Store. Layering those three rules left eight candidate areas in West and North Philadelphia. Candidate 4 is the largest at about 219.68 acres, so it is the recommended site. The map is a screen, so the next step would be checking the parcels inside Candidate 4 for zoning, ownership, and vacancy.

Map 2 · Spatial joins and rates

Crime and Visitors Across Chicago

Raw counts make large Chicago police districts look busier, simply because they cover more ground. To compare districts fairly, homicide incidents and Airbnb listings were assigned to police districts, and the totals were divided by each district's area in square miles. Homicide density is highest in a small group of central and south-side districts. Airbnb review density clusters along the north and central lakefront, while many western and southern districts fall in the lowest classes. Side by side, the two maps show visitor activity and violence concentrated in different parts of the city.

Map 3 · Scale and census data

Same County, Two Different Pictures

Population density in Miami-Dade looks different depending on the units used to measure it. Census data were joined to tract polygons by GEO code, and density was calculated as people per square mile for tracts and then for the much smaller census blocks. The tract map smooths the county into broad zones, while the block map picks up dense pockets and empty blocks that the tracts average away. The pair shows the modifiable areal unit problem, where the size of the units changes the pattern on the map. It also warns against the ecological fallacy, since a dense tract does not mean every resident lives in a crowded block.
